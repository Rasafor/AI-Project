The discipline

1. What AI governance is, and is not

AI governance is the set of decision rights, rules and evidence that lets an organization use AI on purpose: it knows what AI it runs, who answers for each system, how risky each one is, and can prove each one is doing what was approved. It is not an ethics statement, and it is not a committee that reads every prompt. Done well, it makes good projects faster, because teams know the path to yes.

TermWhat it meansWho owns it
GovernanceDecision rights and accountability: who may approve what, on what evidenceAI Council, chaired by the Chief Risk Officer
Risk managementFinding, sizing and treating the ways a system can cause harmGovernance Lead with system owners
ComplianceMeeting specific external obligations (insurance regulators, privacy law)Chief Compliance Officer
Responsible AIThe principles (fair, transparent, accountable, secure) the rules put into practiceEveryone; written down in the policy

Three lines of defense

Red flag in AI output
A governance charter generated by AI that lists principles but names no decision owner, no tier, and no evidence. Ask: "Who can say no, and what would they need to see to say yes?" If the document cannot answer, it is not governance.

The discipline

2. The AI inventory and use-case intake

You cannot govern what you cannot see. The AI inventory is the register of every AI system in use or in build, with an owner, a purpose, a tier and a status. Intake is the front door: one short form, filed before anyone buys, builds or switches on AI. The inventory is the single most-requested artifact by regulators and auditors, so it must be complete before it is pretty.

What counts as an AI system?

Use a broad, testable definition: any system that produces predictions, classifications, recommendations, scores or generated content from data, including vendor products with AI features switched on. Spreadsheets with fixed formulas are out; a vendor CRM whose "smart" lead score is on is in.

Intake fieldWhy it matters
Business owner (a named person)Accountability; a team name cannot be held to account
Purpose and the decision it influencesDrives the impact score; "efficiency" is not a purpose
Who is affected (consumers, employees, neither)Drives exposure and notice duties
Data used, including restricted classesDrives sensitivity and the data approval
Build, buy or vendor featureDecides whether vendor controls apply
Autonomy: who acts on the outputA score that pays a claim is not the same as a score a person reads
Shadow AI is normal; ignoring it is not
Meridian's first proxy-log and expense scan found 2 AI systems nobody had registered (a call-analytics tool and a browser note-taker). Treat discovery as amnesty plus intake, not punishment, or the next one will be hidden better.

3. Risk tiering: proportionate control

Not every AI system deserves the same scrutiny. Risk tiering sorts systems so that a fraud score that triggers investigations gets independent validation and fairness testing, while a coding assistant gets a registration and a policy acknowledgement. Without tiers, governance either strangles low-risk work or waves through high-risk work.

The TrustLine scoring rule

Score = 2 x Impact + Autonomy + Exposure + Data sensitivity      (each 1 to 5, max 25)
Tier 1 >= 19   Tier 2 14-18   Tier 3 9-13   Tier 4 <= 8
Floor: a consequential decision about a consumer with no human review is Tier 1.
Prohibited uses (see policy) are not tiered; they are stopped.

Reviewer's shortcut
Impact is counted twice on purpose. A system can be low-autonomy and still dangerous if its output steers a decision about someone's money. When an AI draft scores impact low, ask what happens to the person if the output is wrong.

5. Controls and gates across the lifecycle

Controls attach to gates: points in a system's life where evidence is checked before it moves on. Gates are fail-closed: missing evidence means "not yet", never "assume fine". Tier decides which controls a gate checks, not whether the gate exists.

GateQuestion it answersEvidence checkedControls at this gate
G0 Intake and tieringIs this AI, who owns it, and how risky is it?Inventory record, tiering worksheet3
G1 Design reviewIs the purpose legitimate, the data allowed and the oversight real?System sheet, data approval, oversight design, vendor clauses6
G2 ValidationDoes it work, fairly and safely, against criteria written before testing?Validation report, fairness report, red-team log5
G3 Go-live approvalIs everything in place to run it and stop it?Council decision, notices, logging, fallback test4
G4 Operate and monitorIs it still doing what was approved?Monitoring, change tickets, incidents, re-assessments6
G5 RetireIs it switched off cleanly, with records kept?Decommission record, data disposal certificate0
Write acceptance criteria before testing
Validation (G2) checks results against criteria written at design (G1). Criteria written after the results are seen will always be met. Ask for the dated criteria document.

6. Fairness and unfair-discrimination testing

An AI system can discriminate without ever seeing a protected attribute, because other inputs act as proxies: ZIP code, address history and credit-based scores can all correlate with race. Fairness testing measures outcomes by group and asks whether any gap is justified by the business purpose and could not be closed by a less discriminatory alternative.

How Meridian tests

  1. Infer group membership in aggregate with BIFSG (surname and geography), because insurers do not collect race. Inferred labels are used only for testing and never attached to a claim.
  2. Compute each group's adverse-outcome rate (for SentinelRisk, the SIU referral rate) and divide it by the reference group's rate.
  3. A ratio above 1.25 (the inverse of the four-fifths screen) is a finding that must be explained or fixed.
  4. Find the driving features, test a less discriminatory alternative, and record the accuracy cost of the fix.
Red flag in AI output
A fairness section that reports overall accuracy only, or says the model is fair "because it does not use race". Both miss proxies entirely. Ask for outcome rates by group, the threshold, and the features driving any gap.

7. Transparency, explainability and human oversight

Transparency tells people AI is involved. Explainability gives the person acting on an output a reason they can check. Human oversight makes sure a person can catch and reverse a wrong output in time. All three fail when they exist only on paper.

Oversight patternWhat the person doesUse it whenMeridian example
Human-in-the-loopApproves every output before it takes effectHigh impact, low volumeSIU investigator decides on every SentinelRisk referral
Human-on-the-loopMonitors outputs and can stop or reverse themHigh volume, reversible, cappedClaimsPilot fast-track payments up to $2,500, sampled daily
Human-over-the-loopSets rules and reviews performance periodicallyLow impactDeskAssist usage reviewed quarterly

Is the oversight real?

Track the override rate: how often people change the AI's output. Near zero usually means rubber-stamping (automation bias); very high means the system is not useful. ClaimsPilot's target band is 3% to 12%.

Ask for the reason, not the weights
A useful explanation is one the reader can act on: "referred because the loss was reported 2 days after policy start and the repair shop is on the watch list", not a chart of feature importance.

8. Third-party and vendor AI

Most enterprise AI is bought, not built. Buying a model does not transfer accountability: regulators hold the insurer responsible for a vendor model's outcomes. Vendor governance is due diligence before signature, contract terms that give you evidence, and monitoring after go-live.

Ask the vendor forWhyWalk away if
Model documentation: purpose, training data sources, known limitsYou must explain the system to regulatorsEverything is "proprietary"
Fairness testing results, and the right to run your own testsYou carry the unfair-discrimination riskThey refuse test access to scored outputs
Notice before material model changesA silent retrain is a new, unapproved systemChanges ship without notice
Data-use limits: no training on your data without consentYour claimants' data must not leave its purposeTerms allow training on customer data by default
Audit rights and incident notice (72 hours)You need evidence when something goes wrongNo audit clause
Red flag in AI output
A vendor assessment that copies the vendor's own marketing claims ("bias-free", "explainable AI") as evidence. Evidence is a test result you can rerun, a contract clause, or a document with a date.

9. Monitoring, incidents and the audit trail

Approval is a snapshot; production is a movie. Models drift when the world changes (a hail season, a new repair-shop network), vendors retrain, and prompts get edited. Monitoring watches inputs, outputs and outcomes against thresholds; incident response decides what happens when a threshold is crossed; the audit trail proves what the system did and who acted.

The dashed line is the amber threshold (PSI 0.1). August crossed it during a hail event; the red-line rule on severe misroutes triggered automatic fallback to human triage within the hour.

SeverityDefinitionResponse
Sev 1Consumer harm at scale, a legal breach, or a regulator inquiryKill switch now; Council chair and Compliance within 1 hour
Sev 2Red threshold crossed or harm to identifiable peopleFallback within 4 hours; root cause in 5 business days
Sev 3Amber threshold or a caught error with no harmFix in the normal backlog; review at the monthly forum
Tamper-evident decision logs
Tier 1 systems log every AI-influenced decision (inputs, model version, output, the human action) in an append-only, hash-chained store kept for 7 years. A regulator question about one claim becomes a lookup, not a reconstruction.

10. Generative AI: what changes

Generative AI adds risks that predictive models do not have: it can state falsehoods fluently, be steered by instructions hidden in a document (prompt injection), leak what it was given, and is used by every employee, not just a model team. The tiering and gates still apply; the tests change.

RiskControlEvidence
Fabricated answersGround answers in approved sources and require citations; refuse when no sourceGolden-set accuracy and citation checks
Prompt injection from documents or emailTreat retrieved text as data; tool permissions limited to the taskRed-team test log
Data leakageEnterprise tenant with no training on prompts; DLP on restricted dataContract clause and DLP rule
Unvetted employee useApproved tools list; acceptable-use rules; blocked consumer toolsProxy logs, policy acknowledgements
Acceptable use in one line
Employees may use approved AI tools for drafting and summarizing; they may not paste claimant, health or payment data into any tool not approved for it, and they own anything they send.

The review lens: approving AI-generated governance work

AI will draft your policies, assessments and model cards in minutes. Your job is to direct it with a precise brief, evaluate the draft against a known standard, and approve only what an examiner, an auditor and a harmed customer would all accept.

CheckQuestion to askReject if
OwnershipIs every control, finding and decision owned by a named person?Owners are teams or "TBD"
TestabilityCould an auditor test each "must" and get a yes or no?Statements use "appropriate", "where possible", "periodically"
EvidenceDoes each claim point to an artifact with a date?Claims rest on assertions or vendor marketing
ProportionalityDo controls scale with tier?Low-risk tools carry the full Tier 1 burden, or the reverse
Failure pathWhat happens when a threshold is crossed or a control fails?Only the approval path is described
ConsistencyDo tiers, counts and names match the inventory?The same system has different tiers in two documents
CurrencyAre regulatory references current and confirmed by Compliance?Citations are vague or undated

A brief that gets a usable first draft

Draft a [document] for [system] at [organization].
Context: [purpose, decision it influences, who is affected, data classes, vendor or in-house].
Tier: [n], scored [impact/autonomy/exposure/sensitivity]. Applicable controls: [ids].
Standards: NIST AI RMF function per control; NAIC AI bulletin themes; our policy [section].
Must include: named owners, dated evidence for every claim, thresholds with red-line actions,
the failure path, open issues. Do not invent test results; mark missing evidence as a finding.
The most common AI drafting failure
Invented evidence: test results, dates and approvals that never happened, written in a confident register. Every number in an assessment must trace to a test run or a source document. If it does not, it is a finding, not a fact.

1. Purpose and authority

This charter establishes the Meridian Mutual Insurance AI Council and the TrustLine AI governance program. The Board Risk Committee delegates to the Council the authority to approve, condition, pause or prohibit any AI system used by the company or by vendors on its behalf. No Tier 1 system may enter production without a Council decision.

All AI systems as defined in the Responsible AI Policy (TL-AG-02): built, bought, or enabled as a feature of a vendor product, used by any employee, contractor or vendor acting for Meridian Mutual Insurance.

6. Escalation

  • A Sev 1 AI incident goes to the Council chair and Compliance within 1 hour.
  • A finding not closed by its due date escalates to the owner's executive at +15 days and the Council at +30 days.
  • A disagreement between the Governance Lead and a system owner on tier goes to the next weekly triage, then the Council.
Quorum and records
Quorum is the chair plus three voting members, including Compliance. Every decision is recorded with the evidence reviewed, conditions, owner and due dates, and any dissent by name.

7. Charter review

Internal Audit assesses the program annually against this charter. The Council reviews the charter every year or after any Sev 1 incident.

1. Purpose and scope

This policy sets the minimum requirements for using AI at Meridian Mutual Insurance. It applies to all employees, contractors and vendors acting on the company's behalf, and to every AI system as defined below.

2. Definition

AI system: any system that produces predictions, classifications, recommendations, scores or generated content from data, including AI features of vendor products. A system with fixed, hand-written rules only is not an AI system.

3. Requirements

RAI-02ProportionateEvery AI system must be tiered and meet the controls for its tier before use.TL-C-05
RAI-03FairTier 1 systems affecting consumers must pass unfair-discrimination testing before use and quarterly.TL-C-20
RAI-04TransparentConsumers and employees must be told when AI materially contributes to a decision about them.TL-C-14

4. Prohibited uses

  • Denying a claim, cancelling or non-renewing a policy without a person reviewing the decision.
  • Using protected characteristics, or inferred ones, as inputs to underwriting, pricing or claims decisions. (Inferred labels are allowed only for aggregate fairness testing.)
  • Emotion recognition or biometric categorization of customers or employees.
  • Uploading claimant, health, payment or employee data to any AI tool not approved for that data class.
  • Generating content that impersonates a real person, or customer communications not identified as from Meridian.

5. Generative AI acceptable use

You mayYou must not
Use DeskAssist and CodeAssist for drafting, summarizing and codingUse consumer AI tools, browser extensions or note-takers for company work
Paste internal, non-restricted information into approved toolsPaste claimant, health, payment or SSN data into any tool not approved for it
Use AI drafts as a starting pointSend AI output to a customer or regulator without reading and owning it

6. Exceptions

Exceptions are requested through intake, must state the compensating control, and expire within 12 months. The AI Council approves exceptions for Tier 1 and 2 systems; the Governance Lead for Tier 3 and 4. Expired exceptions are findings.

Enforcement

1. System and decision

SentinelRisk (TL-AI-02, Tier 1, vendor) scores every claim. Claims scoring above 0.82 are referred to the Special Investigations Unit, where an investigator decides whether to open an investigation. A referral delays payment by a median of 9 days, so it is an adverse outcome for an honest claimant.

2. Method

  • Population: all 58,400 claims scored, Oct 2025 to Sep 2026.
  • Group membership inferred with BIFSG in aggregate; labels kept in a restricted test environment and deleted after the test.
  • Metric: referral rate per group divided by the White group's rate. Threshold 1.25, set in the policy before testing.
  • Driver analysis: drop-one-feature re-scoring on the vendor's test endpoint (contract clause V-04).

3. Results

4. Cause

Two features carried most of the gap: territory_claim_density (claims per 1,000 policies in the claimant's ZIP code), which tracks neighborhood demographics more than fraud, and prior_claims_at_address, which counts claims by anyone at an address and penalizes multi-unit housing.

Accepted cost
Option C finds an estimated $0.5M less fraud a year. Nadia Sorensen (Head of SIU) and Diane Castellanos (CRO) accepted this on 2026-09-22 as the less discriminatory alternative that still meets the business purpose.

Approved with conditions by the AI Council, 2026-09-22.

3. Response runbook

  1. Detect: alert, complaint, employee report or audit. Open an AI incident ticket and assign a severity.
  2. Contain: fallback or kill switch per the threshold table. Containment comes before diagnosis.
  3. Assess harm: query the decision log for every affected decision; list affected people.
  4. Remediate: correct affected decisions (re-review, supplemental payment, apology letter).
  5. Notify: Compliance decides regulator and consumer notice; Privacy Counsel decides breach notice.
  6. Learn: root cause, control change, regression test, and a Council note within the due date.

Glossary

TermMeaning
Adverse outcomeA result that harms the person it is about: a referral, a denial, a higher price, a delay.
AI CouncilThe executive body that approves, conditions, pauses or prohibits Tier 1 AI systems.
AI inventoryThe register of every AI system with owner, purpose, tier and status.
AI systemAnything that produces predictions, classifications, recommendations, scores or generated content from data, including AI features in vendor products.
Automation biasThe tendency of people to accept a system's output without checking it.
BIFSGBayesian Improved First name and Surname Geocoding: a method to infer race and ethnicity in aggregate for fairness testing.
Decision logAn append-only record of each AI-influenced decision: inputs, version, output and the human action.
DriftA change in a system's inputs or outcomes after it was approved.
Fail-closedA gate that treats missing evidence as "no" rather than "assume fine".
Fairness thresholdThe ratio of a group's adverse-outcome rate to the reference group's above which a finding is raised (1.25 at Meridian).
Floor ruleA rule that sets a minimum tier regardless of score, such as consequential consumer decisions with no human review.
GateA lifecycle point where evidence is checked before a system moves on (G0 to G5).
Human-in-the-loopA person approves every output before it takes effect.
Human-on-the-loopOutputs take effect, and a person monitors and can reverse them.
IntakeThe required registration step before any AI system is bought, built or switched on.
ISO/IEC 42001International, certifiable standard for an AI management system.
Kill switchA tested way to stop an AI system and fall back to a manual process.
Material changeA change to model, prompt, vendor version or data source that could change outcomes; it re-enters intake.
Model cardA short document stating a model's intended use, limits, performance and oversight.
NAIC AI bulletinThe NAIC Model Bulletin on the Use of AI Systems by Insurers, adopted by many US states.
NIST AI RMFNIST AI Risk Management Framework 1.0, organized as GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE and MANAGE.
Override rateThe share of AI outputs a person changes; a test of whether oversight is real.
Prompt injectionInstructions hidden in input content that try to steer a generative AI system.
ProxyAn input that stands in for a protected characteristic, such as ZIP code for race.
PSIPopulation Stability Index: a measure of how much an input distribution has shifted.
Risk tierThe level of scrutiny a system gets, set by a scoring rule (Tier 1 to 4, or prohibited).
Shadow AIAI in use without registration or approval.
