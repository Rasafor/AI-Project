Business Analyst Field Guide

The Discipline

Why Business Analysis Exists

AI can generate a requirements document in thirty seconds. It cannot yet tell you whether the requirement is correct — whether it reflects what the business actually needs, whether it is testable, whether it conflicts with another requirement three documents away, or whether “the system should be fast” means 200ms or 2 seconds. That gap is why business analysis exists, and it is exactly the gap an AI Solution Architect is paid to close.

Business analysis is the discipline of translating a fuzzy business need into a precise, testable specification — before expensive engineering work begins. Skipping it does not remove the ambiguity; it just moves the cost of resolving that ambiguity from a cheap conversation on a whiteboard to an expensive rewrite in production. Every dollar spent clarifying a requirement before code is written is, by long-standing industry experience, worth roughly ten dollars saved after.

What happens without it

An AI agent is asked to “build a system that reduces food waste.” It ships a dashboard. Nobody defined what “reduce” means, by how much, by when, measured how, or who is accountable if it doesn’t happen. Six months later, the initiative is judged a failure — not because the AI built the wrong dashboard, but because no one specified the right one.

As an AI Solution Architect, you sit between business stakeholders and AI-generated technical work. Business analysis is your shared vocabulary with both sides — the thing that lets you catch a bad requirement before it becomes a bad system.

The Discipline

The 20% an Architect Must Know

You do not need to become a full-time business analyst. You need the subset of the discipline that lets you evaluate AI output critically. That subset is seven ideas.

1. Functional vs. non-functional requirements

A functional requirement describes what the system does — a behavior, a feature, an action a user can take. A non-functional requirement (NFR) describes how well the system must do it — performance, security, availability, scalability, usability, compliance. AI-generated requirements sets are almost always functional-heavy and NFR-thin, because functional behavior is easier to imagine from a prompt than an operational constraint. An architect’s first pass on any AI-drafted BRD should be: where are the NFRs, and are they measurable numbers or adjectives?

Adjective, not a requirement

“The system shall be fast and reliable.”

Measurable, testable

“The prep-list screen shall render in under 2 seconds at the 95th percentile on standard restaurant Wi-Fi, and the platform shall maintain 99.5% uptime during operating hours.”

2. User stories

A user story captures a requirement from the perspective of the person who needs it, in the form As a <role>, I want <capability>, so that <benefit>. Its value is that it forces every requirement to name a real beneficiary and a real reason — a story with no clear “so that” is usually a requirement nobody actually asked for.

3. Acceptance criteria (Given / When / Then)

A user story states intent; acceptance criteria state the test. The Given/When/Then format — Given a starting state, When an action occurs, Then an outcome is guaranteed — turns a story into something a QA engineer (human or AI) can pass or fail objectively. A story without acceptance criteria cannot be verified, only debated.

4. MoSCoW prioritization

Must have (the initiative fails without it), Should have (important, painful to omit, not fatal), Could have (nice, cut first under pressure), Won’t have this time (explicitly, deliberately, out of scope — written down so nobody re-litigates it mid-project). MoSCoW only works if the distribution is honest. If everything is a Must, nothing is.

5. Personas

A persona is not a demographic profile. “35-year-old general manager, married, drives a Honda” tells you nothing useful about what to build. A usable persona names goals (what this person is trying to accomplish) and frustrations (what currently gets in the way) — because those two things are what a requirement should actually respond to.

6. Use cases

A use case is a structured description of an actor achieving a goal through a system, including the main (happy) path and the alternate and exception paths — what happens when a system is unavailable, when a user does something unexpected, or when a business rule blocks the obvious action. An AI-drafted requirements set that only covers the happy path is not describing a system; it is describing a demo.

7. Traceability

Every requirement should trace forward to a test case that verifies it and backward to a business objective that justifies it. A requirement with no traceable objective is an orphan requirement — someone’s pet feature that slipped in without anyone asking why it exists. A Requirements Traceability Matrix (RTM) is the tool that makes orphans visible.

The Discipline

Good vs. Bad Requirements

Every AI-drafted requirement should pass four checks before an architect approves it: is it specific, is it testable, is it prioritized honestly, and is it traceable to a business objective. The table below shows the same requirement before and after that review.

#Bad (as AI often drafts it)Why it failsGood (after architect review)
1The system should reduce food waste.No number, no owner, no deadline — unfalsifiable.The forecasting platform shall reduce food-waste cost as a % of food cost from 11% to 7.7% within two quarters of full rollout, measured against monthly P&L food-cost reporting.
2The app should be easy to use.“Easy” is not observable or testable.90% of General Managers shall complete the prep-list review workflow without support after one 30-minute onboarding session, measured via a post-onboarding task-completion survey.
3The system must support all future integrations.Unbounded scope; “all” and “future” cannot be tested.The platform architecture shall support scaling to 120 locations without redesign, to accommodate the defined Phase 2 franchise rollout.
4Everything in this document is a Must-have.Priority inflation — signals no real trade-off analysis occurred.16 Must, 12 Should, 4 Could, 2 Won’t-this-phase, each independently justified against a business objective (see the BRD).
5The system should handle errors gracefully.“Gracefully” is undefined; no fallback behavior specified.If the POS integration is unreachable, the system shall fall back to a 7-day rolling average forecast, flag the output “Degraded,” and alert the IT Integration Lead within 5 minutes.
The Discipline

KPIs & Measurable Outcomes

A requirement without a KPI behind it is an opinion. Every business objective in this guide’s example initiative is backed by a specific, currently-measured baseline and a target — not an aspiration. See the KPI tiles in the Executive Summary and the ROI table in the Vision & Business Case for the worked numbers. When you review an AI-generated business case, insist on the same three things every time: a baseline (where are we now), a target (where must we be, and by when), and a measurement method (how will anyone know).

The Discipline

The Architect’s Review Lens

When an AI agent hands you a requirements artifact, run this checklist before you approve it. This is the actual lens applied to every document in this guide.

CheckQuestion to askRed flag
SpecificityCould two different engineers build two different things from this sentence?Adjectives without numbers (“fast,” “secure,” “user-friendly”)
TestabilityIs there a Given/When/Then or a pass/fail test that proves this is done?Acceptance criteria that restate the requirement instead of testing it
Priority honestyWhat is this a Must relative to — would the initiative genuinely fail without it?More than ~50% of items marked Must; zero items marked Won’t
TraceabilityWhich business objective does this serve? Which test case verifies it?Requirements with no linked objective (orphans) in the RTM
Unhappy pathsWhat does this use case do when the dependency is down, or the user does the wrong thing?Use cases with only a main flow, no alternate or exception flow
NFR presenceAre performance, security, availability, and compliance addressed, or only features?A BRD that is 100% functional requirements
Stakeholder completenessDoes the list include people who are affected but have low power (e.g., front-line staff)?Only executives and IT named as stakeholders
Scope boundaryIs out-of-scope stated explicitly, not just left unmentioned?No “out of scope” section at all
Rule of thumb

If you can approve an AI-generated requirement without being able to explain, in one sentence, how someone would prove it was met, send it back.

UC-01 — Generate Daily Prep & Reorder Forecast

Primary ActorForecasting Engine (system)
Secondary ActorsHead Chef, General Manager, Procurement System
TriggerNightly batch schedule (11:30 PM local) or manual re-run by a GM
PreconditionsLocation onboarded with ≥90 days POS history (or using rolling-average fallback); POS integration active

Main Flow

  1. Scheduler triggers the nightly forecast job for the location.
  2. System pulls the last 24 hours of POS transactions plus the rolling historical window.
  3. System pulls current on-hand inventory counts.
  4. System pulls calendar flags (weather, local events) if present.
  5. Forecasting model generates predicted order volume and mix by daypart and menu item.
  6. System calculates required prep quantities per item using recipe yield data.
  7. System compares forecasted ingredient usage to on-hand inventory plus safety stock.
  8. Where usage exceeds inventory, system drafts a purchase order.
  9. System publishes the prep list and confidence indicators to the location dashboard by 5:00 AM.
  10. Head Chef and GM review the prep list at shift start.

Alternate Flows

  • A1 — Manual re-run: GM triggers a re-run mid-afternoon after learning of a same-day event; steps 2–9 repeat using the latest data and are timestamped as a same-day update.
  • A2 — New menu item, insufficient history: System detects fewer than 14 days of history for an item; forecast uses a category-average proxy and is flagged Low Confidence.

Exception / Unhappy Paths

  • E1 — POS data unavailable: Step 2 fails; system falls back to a 7-day rolling average, flags output “Degraded,” and alerts the IT Integration Lead within 5 minutes.
  • E2 — Inventory system unavailable: Step 3 fails; prep list still generates without auto-drafted POs; flagged “Inventory sync failed — manual PO review required” and Procurement is notified.
  • E3 — Forecast job exceeds time window: If runtime exceeds 90 minutes, the job is terminated, yesterday’s successful forecast is republished as “Stale,” and an incident ticket is auto-filed.

Business Rules: Safety stock = 1.5 days of forecasted usage (configurable per ingredient class). Confidence <70% triggers a Low Confidence flag.
Related Requirements: FR-01, FR-02, FR-05, FR-07, FR-17, NFR-01, NFR-03, NFR-13.

UC-02 — Manager Overrides AI-Suggested Prep Quantity

Primary ActorGeneral Manager
Secondary ActorsForecasting Engine, Regional Operations Director (reviewer)
TriggerGM reviews the prep list and disagrees with a quantity
PreconditionsPrep list published for the location/day; GM authenticated with GM-role access

Main Flow

  1. GM opens today’s prep list.
  2. GM selects an item and enters a revised quantity.
  3. System requires a reason code (Local event, Known regular, Weather, Other).
  4. GM submits the override.
  5. System saves the override with user ID, timestamp, original value, new value, and reason code.
  6. System recalculates dependent ingredient usage and updates any draft PO.
  7. Override is flagged for weekly aggregate model-retraining review.

Alternate Flows

  • A1 — Bulk override: GM applies a percentage adjustment across all items ahead of a known citywide event; logged as a single bulk-override event.

Exception / Unhappy Paths

  • E1 — Missing reason code: Submission blocked with inline validation: “Reason code required for all overrides.”
  • E2 — Override below safety-stock floor: Allowed, but displays an amber warning callout and requires a secondary confirmation click.
  • E3 — Override after prep cutoff: Accepted for logging/learning only: “Too late to affect today’s prep — will apply to tomorrow’s forecast only.”

Related Requirements: FR-03, FR-04.

UC-03 — System Detects Anomalous Order Pattern and Triggers Alert

Primary ActorForecasting Engine (anomaly-detection component)
Secondary ActorsGeneral Manager, Regional Operations Director
TriggerReal-time order volume deviates from forecast by more than the anomaly threshold (default ±25%)
PreconditionsLocation live on the platform with an active real-time POS feed

Main Flow

  1. System continuously compares actual order pace to forecast throughout each daypart.
  2. System detects actual volume exceeding forecast by >25% at the 1-hour mark.
  3. System classifies the anomaly (volume spike vs. mix shift).
  4. System sends an in-app and SMS alert with a suggested action.
  5. GM acknowledges or dismisses the alert.
  6. System logs the anomaly, GM response, and end-of-day actual-vs-forecast variance.

Alternate Flows

  • A1 — Negative anomaly: Volume >25% below forecast triggers a “Slow pace” notice with a labor-adjustment prompt instead of a reorder trigger.

Exception / Unhappy Paths

  • E1 — Alert fatigue guard: After 3+ alerts in 2 hours for one location, further alerts are suppressed and a single consolidated alert escalates to the Regional Operations Director.
  • E2 — SMS delivery failure: Falls back to in-app + email; failure is logged for the IT Integration Lead.

Related Requirements: FR-16, NFR-13.

Current-State & Future-State Process

SmartServe AI — Demand Forecasting & Dynamic Prep Planning Platform

Harvest & Hearth Restaurant Group

Document IDPROC-HH-2026-001
Version1.1
OwnerAndre Kowalski & Priya Raman
StatusApproved

Current State (As-Is)

Prep planning today is manual and backward-looking. Waste is only visible at month-end, roughly 30–45 days after it happens — long after a Regional Director could have intervened.

  1. Head Chef pulls a “same day last week” sales report from the POS (manual export).
  2. Chef estimates today’s prep quantities by gut-feel adjustment.
  3. Chef writes the prep list on paper.
  4. Kitchen staff prep to the paper list.
  5. GM performs a visual walk-in inventory check each morning.
  6. If a shortage is noticed, GM calls the supplier directly — often a same-day rush order.
  7. Waste is logged inconsistently at week’s end (most locations estimate rather than weigh).
  8. At month-end, Finance reports food-cost variance; Regional Ops follows up on outliers 30–45 days after the fact.

Future State (To-Be)

SmartServe AI moves every one of the current state’s reactive steps to a proactive, data-driven equivalent, and compresses the waste-visibility loop from months to days.

  1. System pulls POS, inventory, and calendar data overnight.
  2. AI generates the prep list and confidence flags by 5:00 AM.
  3. Chef reviews the list on an in-kitchen tablet; prep begins against system-driven quantities.
  4. System auto-drafts POs where inventory gaps are forecasted; GM approves with one tap.
  5. Real-time anomaly detection alerts the GM same-day if actual pace deviates from forecast.
  6. Waste is logged via an in-kitchen scale/scan step, feeding the dashboard directly.
  7. Regional Ops sees a live weekly (not monthly) food-waste dashboard ranked by location.
  8. Finance reconciles modeled savings to actual P&L monthly, with an auditable variance explanation.
