The Discipline

Why Solution Architecture Exists

An AI agent can generate a working system design in minutes: pick some services, draw some boxes, wire them with REST calls. What it cannot yet reliably do is tell you whether that design will survive a 37x traffic spike during a hurricane, whether a regulator can reconstruct why a specific claim was auto-approved three years after the fact, or whether the team has any realistic chance of operating the message broker it just recommended. That gap — between a design that runs in a demo and a design that survives production, audits, and scale — is why solution architecture exists, and it is exactly the gap an AI Solution Architect is paid to close.

Solution architecture is the discipline of translating approved business requirements into a technical structure that satisfies not just what the system must do, but how well it must do it, under real load, real failure conditions, and real regulatory scrutiny — before expensive engineering work begins. Skipping it does not remove the risk; it moves the cost of discovering a bad architectural choice from a cheap whiteboard conversation to an expensive production incident or a failed compliance exam.

What happens without it

An AI agent is asked to “build a claims intake system.” It ships a monolith that calls a fraud-scoring API synchronously on every request. Nobody defined what happens when 3,000 claims arrive in four hours during a hurricane instead of the usual 80 a day, or what happens when the fraud vendor’s API times out. The system falls over during the exact event it exists to help with — not because the AI wrote bad code, but because no one specified the load it had to survive or the failure modes it had to tolerate.

As an AI Solution Architect, you sit between business stakeholders, compliance, operations, and AI-generated technical work. Solution architecture is your shared vocabulary with all three — the thing that lets you catch a fragile design before it becomes a production incident.

The Discipline

The 20% an Architect Must Know

You do not need to become a full-time systems engineer. You need the subset of the discipline that lets you evaluate AI-generated technical designs critically. That subset is eight ideas.

1. Quality attributes (non-functional requirements)

A quality attribute — also called a non-functional requirement (NFR) — describes how well a system must behave: availability, latency, throughput, scalability, security, auditability, recoverability, usability, maintainability, cost. AI-generated architectures are almost always feature-heavy and quality-attribute-thin, because a feature is easy to imagine from a prompt and a quality attribute requires someone to have already thought about failure. An architect’s first pass on any AI-drafted design should be: where are the numbers, and would two different engineers build two different things from this sentence?

Adjective, not a target

“The claims platform shall be highly available and fast.”

Measurable, testable

“The FNOL intake channel shall maintain 99.95% uptime, 24/7/365, and shall acknowledge a submission in under 2 seconds at the 95th percentile.”

2. Architecture styles — monolith vs. services vs. event-driven

A monolith deploys the whole system as one unit — simple to reason about and operate, but every part scales and releases together, and one bad deploy can take down everything. A service-based (or microservices) architecture splits the system into independently deployable units communicating over the network — each part scales and releases on its own schedule, at the cost of operational complexity and network failure modes that don’t exist in a monolith. An event-driven architecture decouples producers and consumers through an event backbone (a message broker or log) instead of direct calls — ideal when work is naturally asynchronous and volume is spiky, at the cost of harder-to-trace request flows and eventual, not immediate, consistency. Most real systems are a hybrid: synchronous APIs where a human is waiting for an answer, asynchronous events where the work can happen in the background. Neither style is “best” — each is justified against a specific load profile and failure tolerance, never chosen by default.

StyleBest fit when…Watch out for…
MonolithSmall team, low/steady load, simplicity matters more than independent scalingOne slow query can degrade the whole system; releases are all-or-nothing
Service-based / microservicesDifferent parts of the system have very different load or release cadencesOperational overhead, distributed-systems failure modes, network latency between calls
Event-drivenWork is naturally asynchronous, volume is bursty, multiple consumers need the same dataHarder to trace a request end-to-end; requires an event backbone someone must operate

3. The C4 model

C4 gives you four nested zoom levels for describing a system, so an AI-generated diagram can be evaluated at the right altitude instead of one confusing tangle of boxes. Context: the system as one box, its users, and the external systems it talks to — a diagram a non-technical stakeholder can read. Container: zoom into the system to show its major deployable units (services, apps, data stores) and how they call each other. Component: zoom into one container to show its internal building blocks. Code: class-level detail — generally left to the codebase itself, not a document. This guide’s C4 Diagrams document works through Context, Container, and Component for the worked example.

4. Architecture Decision Records (ADRs)

An ADR captures one significant decision: the context that forced it, the decision itself, the options that were rejected and why, and the resulting consequences (both positive and negative). Its value is almost entirely in the rejected options — without them, an ADR is just a decision with no defense, and six months later someone re-litigates the same argument from scratch. An AI-generated architecture with no ADRs, or with ADRs that list no rejected alternatives, has not actually documented a decision — it has documented a preference.

5. Trade-off analysis

Every non-trivial technology choice is a trade-off, not a verdict. A defensible trade-off analysis scores each option against weighted criteria that matter to this system (not generic “best practice”), and — critically — states what the winning option costs, not just what it wins. “We chose Kafka because it’s powerful” is not a trade-off analysis. “We chose Kafka because it scored highest on replay and fan-out, at the cost of a new operational skill the team doesn’t have yet” is. See the Technology Trade-off Matrix for four worked comparisons.

6. Key patterns

PatternWhat it solves
API Gateway / BFF (Backend-for-Frontend)One stable entry point for client apps, hiding internal service topology and version changes from the caller
Circuit breakerStops calling a failing downstream dependency after N failures, so one slow/broken system doesn’t cascade into an outage of everything that calls it
BulkheadIsolates resource pools (threads, connections) per dependency, so one overloaded integration can’t starve the others
SagaCoordinates a multi-step transaction across services without a single distributed lock, using compensating actions if a later step fails

Strangler figIncrementally routes traffic from a legacy system to a new one, one capability at a time, instead of a risky big-bang rewrite
CQRS (Command Query Responsibility Segregation)Separates the write model from the read model when their scaling or shape needs diverge sharply

7. Fitness functions

A fitness function is an automated, repeatable check that a specific architectural characteristic still holds — run in CI, not verified by memory or a design review six months ago. It turns an architectural intent into something that fails a build instead of quietly rotting. Three fitness functions for this guide’s example: (1) a load test that fails if p95 triage latency exceeds 60 seconds; (2) a static-analysis rule that fails the build if a new service calls the legacy Policy Administration System synchronously in the request path (violates ADR-004); (3) a nightly job that fails if any AI-assisted triage decision in the audit log is missing its model version or reason code (violates NFR-08).

8. Build vs. buy

Every significant capability forces a build-vs-buy decision, and an AI agent will default to whichever it was more recently trained on, not whichever is actually right for this system. The framework: time-to-value (how long until this works), differentiation (is this something that makes the business win, or table stakes), control & compliance (can we live with data leaving our boundary, can we get the explainability a regulator will demand), and total cost of ownership (license/usage fees vs. the fully-loaded cost of building and operating it ourselves). See ADR-006 for this framework applied to the fraud-scoring engine.

The Discipline

Good vs. Bad Architecture

Every AI-drafted architecture statement should pass four checks before an architect approves it: is it measurable, is it justified against real load, does it state a rejected alternative, and does it state a cost, not just a win. The table below shows the same statement before and after that review.

The Discipline

KPIs & Architecture Metrics

An architecture without KPIs behind it is an opinion about boxes and arrows. Two families of metric matter here. Initiative KPIs measure whether the business problem is actually getting solved — see the KPI tiles in the Architecture Package Summary. Architecture health KPIs measure whether the system itself stays maintainable over time — the four DORA metrics are the standard set: deployment frequency (how often can we safely ship), lead time for changes (commit to production), change failure rate (% of deploys that cause a production incident), and mean time to restore (MTTR, how fast can we recover). When you review an AI-generated architecture, insist on the same three things every time for each quality attribute: a baseline (where are we now), a target (where must we be, and by when), and a verification method (how will anyone know, and how often is it checked).

The Architect’s Review Lens

When an AI agent hands you an architecture artifact, run this checklist before you approve it. This is the actual lens applied to every document in this guide.

CheckQuestion to askRed flag
MeasurabilityDoes every quality attribute have a number and a verification method?Adjectives without numbers (“fast,” “secure,” “scalable”)
Load justificationIs the architecture style justified against a stated baseline and peak load?A style choice with no load numbers anywhere near it
Decision defensibilityDoes every significant decision have an ADR with rejected options?ADRs that state a decision but list no alternatives considered
Honest trade-offsDoes the winning option in any comparison state what it costs, not just what it wins?A trade-off matrix where the recommended option has no listed weaknesses
Failure-path coverageWhat does this do when a dependency is down, times out, or returns garbage?Sequence diagrams with only the happy path, no exception flow
Source-of-truth clarityIs it clear which system owns which data, and is there exactly one?Two systems that could both claim to be authoritative for the same record
Security & compliance presenceAre PII handling, retention, and auditability addressed, or only features?An architecture document silent on data classification or retention
Over-engineering checkDoes the complexity match the actual load, or is it sized for hypothetical future scale?15 microservices for a system processing 80 transactions a day
Rule of thumb

If you can approve an AI-generated architecture decision without being able to state, in one sentence, what alternative it rejected and why, send it back.

CompanyMeridian Mutual Insurance — a regional property & casualty insurer headquartered in Raleigh, North Carolina. Approximately 1.1 million policies in force; $640M in annual written premium.
IndustryInsurance (Property & Casualty)
InitiativeClaimsPilot AI — an AI-assisted First Notice of Loss (FNOL) intake, triage, and straight-through processing platform

ClaimsPilot AI — FNOL Intake, Triage & Straight-Through Processing Platform

1. Purpose

This document specifies the target solution architecture for ClaimsPilot AI, an AI-assisted First Notice of Loss (FNOL) intake, triage, and straight-through processing platform for Meridian Mutual Insurance. Business requirements for this initiative are already gathered and approved; this document begins at the architecture phase and is the basis for detailed design, AI-agent-assisted implementation, and go-live readiness review.

2. Business Context & Problem Statement

FNOL intake today is manual and routed by simple round-robin, independent of claim complexity or fraud risk. Average time-to-first-adjuster-contact is 26 hours; low-complexity claims that could be settled same-day instead take an average of 9 days; fraud referral catches only about 40% of eventually-confirmed fraud, often after payment has gone out. ClaimsPilot AI targets all three problems at once: faster routing, faster settlement for simple claims, and earlier fraud detection — without compromising the auditability a state Department of Insurance market-conduct exam requires.

3. Scope

In scope: FNOL intake across web, mobile, and call-center channels; document and call-transcript extraction; severity and fraud-risk triage; straight-through processing for claims under $2,500 meeting confidence and risk thresholds; adjuster and SIU case routing and assignment; claimant and adjuster notifications; payment orchestration for approved straight-through claims; a full, immutable audit trail.

Out of scope (this phase): auto-approval above the $2,500 straight-through ceiling (Phase 2 candidate, pending 12-month pilot results); the full claims lifecycle beyond initial routing (litigation, subrogation, appeals handling); non-P&C lines of business; agent/broker-facing tooling.

4. Architecture Style & Justification

ClaimsPilot AI adopts a hybrid event-driven / service-based architecture: synchronous REST APIs at the two human-facing edges (the claimant FNOL portal and the adjuster/SIU workbench), and an asynchronous event backbone (Kafka via Confluent Cloud) connecting the intake→extraction→triage→routing pipeline. This is justified directly against load: baseline volume is approximately 80 FNOL submissions per day, but a declared catastrophe event (hurricane, hailstorm) can drive volume to roughly 3,000 submissions in a single day — a 37x surge that must be absorbed within 4 hours of the event being declared, without degrading the adjuster workbench’s own responsiveness. A monolith has one scaling unit, so a catastrophe-event surge on the intake path would degrade or take down the human-facing workbench alongside it. An event-driven pipeline lets the ingestion tier scale independently of everything downstream, and lets each pipeline stage absorb backpressure without cascading failure. See ADR-001 and ADR-005 for the full decision record, including rejected alternatives.

5. System Context

ClaimsPilot AI sits between three types of human actor (claimants, adjusters, SIU investigators) and seven external systems it must integrate with: the Policy Administration System (coverage/eligibility lookup), the Core Claims System of Record (the authoritative claims ledger), the Telephony/Call Center Platform (call transcripts), a Payment Processor (ACH disbursement), a Notification Gateway (email/SMS), the SIU Case Management tool, and the enterprise Data Warehouse (regulatory and analytical reporting). See the C4 Context Diagram for the full picture.

6. Container View

Inside the ClaimsPilot AI system boundary, the architecture comprises: a claimant-facing FNOL portal and an adjuster/SIU workbench (both single-page apps behind a shared API Gateway/BFF); an Intake Service; a Document & Transcript Extraction Service; a Triage & Fraud Scoring Service; a Case Routing & Assignment Service; a Notification Service; a Payment Orchestration Service; a Policy Lookup Adapter; and three data stores — a Claims Case Store (in-flight state only, see Section 7), an ML Model Registry & Feature Store, and an append-only Audit & Explainability Log. All pipeline stages communicate through the Kafka event backbone. See the C4 Container Diagram.

7. Data Architecture

The Policy Administration System and Core Claims System of Record remain the systems of truth for policy and claims data respectively — ClaimsPilot AI does not duplicate them. The Claims Case Store (PostgreSQL) holds only in-flight claim state between FNOL receipt and final routing decision; once a routing decision is made, the authoritative record is written back to the Core Claims System of Record via its claims-intake API, and the case store retains a pointer plus the full decision audit trail rather than a second copy of the record. See ADR-004 for why, including a rejected alternative that failed under load testing.

The pipeline communicates via four typed events on the Kafka backbone: FNOLReceived, ExtractionCompleted, TriageDecisionMade, and CaseResolved. Every event and every downstream decision is written to the append-only Audit & Explainability Log, retained for 7 years per state Department of Insurance record-retention requirements (see NFR-08 in the NFR Spec).

9. Security Architecture

PII fields (Social Security Number, driver’s license number) are tokenized at the Intake Service before any downstream service — including the external fraud-scoring vendor — ever sees them; detokenization is restricted to the write-back to the Core Claims System of Record, under role-based access control. All data is encrypted at rest with AES-256 and in transit with TLS 1.2 or higher. Access to PO-approval-equivalent actions (straight-through override, SIU case creation) is restricted by role, and every access is logged. Secrets are held in a managed vault, never in code, configuration files, or logs. See NFR-07 and NFR-09.

10. Deployment View

Services deploy as containers on managed Kubernetes across two AWS regions — primary us-east-1, disaster-recovery us-west-2 — with per-service auto-scaling groups and blue/green releases to support independent, zero-downtime deploys (see NFR-14). Confluent Cloud runs multi-availability-zone within the primary region. The Claims Case Store replicates synchronously within-region and asynchronously to the DR region, consistent with the recoverability targets in NFR-10.

ADR-001 · Adopt a hybrid event-driven / service-based architecture over a monolith

Accepted
Related: NFR-01, NFR-03, NFR-06 · Referenced by: SAD §4

Context

The existing Core Claims System of Record is a legacy monolith on a quarterly release cycle. ClaimsPilot AI must absorb a 37x catastrophe-event surge (80/day baseline to 3,000/day peak, within 4 hours) on its intake path without degrading the adjuster/SIU workbench, and must iterate on fraud/severity models far faster than a quarterly cycle allows.

Decision

Introduce ClaimsPilot AI as a new bounded system that writes decisions back to the legacy system of record. Internally, structure it as a small set of independently deployable services connected by an event backbone for the ingestion→extraction→triage→routing pipeline, with synchronous REST APIs only at the two human-facing edges (claimant portal, adjuster workbench).

Rejected Options

  • Extend the legacy monolith directly. Rejected — its quarterly release cycle cannot support the weekly iteration the fraud/severity models require, and a catastrophe-event surge on intake would risk degrading the same system adjusters rely on to work existing claims.
  • Full microservices (15+ services). Rejected as over-engineering — the operational overhead of that many independently-operated services is not justified for a system processing 80–3,000 claims/day.

Consequences

Positive: independent scaling of the ingestion tier; weekly model iteration becomes possible. Negative: two integration points to maintain against the legacy system of record; the team must learn event-driven debugging patterns it hasn’t needed before.

ADR-002 · Human-in-the-loop straight-through processing for the pilot phase

Accepted
Related: NFR-10, NFR-11 · Referenced by: Risk Register R9

Context

Fully automated claim settlement without any human review carries regulatory and reputational risk if the triage model is wrong — a paid claim is far harder to unwind than an unpaid one.

Decision

Auto-approve only claims under $2,500 with triage confidence ≥0.92 and zero fraud-risk flags. Every auto-approval is logged with full explainability and 10% of them are sampled weekly for adjuster QA review. All other claims route to a human.

Rejected Options

  • Fully automated approval up to $10,000. Rejected — Compliance flagged this as premature for a first release with no production track record for the model.
  • No automation; AI only advises the adjuster. Rejected — does not move the needle on the 9-day average settlement time for simple claims, the initiative’s primary cycle-time objective.

Consequences

Positive: financial exposure to model error is bounded to $2,500 per claim; the design is regulator-defensible from day one. Negative: initial automation rate is estimated at only 18% of volume, well below the eventual 35% target — the ramp is visible in the pilot trend chart in the Trade-off Matrix.

ADR-003 · Managed Kafka (Confluent Cloud) as the event backbone

Accepted
Related: NFR-02, NFR-04, NFR-12 · Referenced by: Trade-off Matrix §A

Context

The pipeline needs ordered, replayable events with multiple independent consumers (extraction, triage, audit log, data warehouse) reading the same stream, plus the ability to absorb a catastrophe-event burst without losing events.

Decision

Adopt Confluent Cloud managed Kafka as the backbone. Each pipeline stage publishes and consumes typed events (FNOLReceived, ExtractionCompleted, TriageDecisionMade, CaseResolved).

Rejected Options

  • Point-to-point REST calls between services. Rejected — creates N-to-N coupling, no replay capability, and no backpressure protection during a surge.
  • AWS SQS/SNS. Rejected — no native long-term replay/log semantics for audit reconstruction, and multi-consumer fan-out requires extra plumbing SNS doesn’t provide out of the box.
  • Azure Service Bus. Rejected — no compelling advantage over Kafka for this use case, and the organization’s cloud footprint is AWS-primary. See the full scoring in the Trade-off Matrix.

Consequences

Positive: replay supports audit reconstruction and reprocessing after a model fix; clean fan-out to the data warehouse. Negative: the team must operate/monitor a new component class (mitigated by choosing a managed offering); adds a baseline cost of roughly $1,800/month.

ADR-004 · Legacy systems remain sources of truth; ClaimsPilot AI holds only in-flight case state

Accepted
Related: Data Architecture §7 · Referenced by: Risk Register R6

Context

Building a second copy of policy or claims data risks that copy drifting from the real source of truth over time — a well-known and expensive failure mode.

Decision

The Claims Case Store (PostgreSQL) holds only claim state that is in-flight, not yet finalized. On a final routing decision, the authoritative record is written back to the Core Claims System of Record via its claims-intake API; the case store retains a pointer and the full audit trail, not a duplicate record.

Rejected Options

  • Build ClaimsPilot AI as a new system of record. Rejected — massive, unjustified scope increase, redundant with an existing compliant system.
  • No local store; call the legacy system synchronously at every pipeline step. Rejected on evidence — a load test showed the legacy Policy Administration System’s API p95 exceeding 4 seconds under 10x load, which would have made the whole pipeline dependent on a component that cannot sustain the required read/write pattern.

Consequences

Positive: single source of truth is preserved; blast radius of any ClaimsPilot AI defect is contained. Negative: requires a reconciliation job to detect and alert on write-back failures — an added operational component.

ADR-005 · Synchronous edges, asynchronous pipeline (hybrid interaction style)

Accepted
Related: NFR-02, NFR-03 · Referenced by: SAD §4, Sequence Diagrams

Context

Claimant and adjuster interactions need immediate, predictable responses. The multi-stage AI pipeline (extraction, scoring, routing) has variable, sometimes multi-second-to-minute latency — especially where LLM inference or human SIU review is involved.

Decision

Claimant- and adjuster-facing APIs are synchronous REST via the API Gateway/BFF, using a “submitted, we’ll notify you” pattern beyond the initial FNOL acknowledgment. The pipeline itself is fully event-driven, decoupling extraction/triage from the human request/response cycle.

Rejected Options

  • Fully synchronous end-to-end (block until triage completes). Rejected — extraction and triage can take up to 60 seconds; blocking a form submission on that is a broken UX and fragile under load.
  • Fully asynchronous, including the claimant’s submission acknowledgment (webhook-only). Rejected — claimants reasonably expect an immediate confirmation number when they file a claim.

Consequences

Positive: predictable UX at the human-facing edges; pipeline stages scale and fail independently. Negative: requires a notification mechanism to close the loop with the claimant, adding a dependency on the Notification Gateway.

ADR-006 · Buy a commercial fraud-scoring engine for the pilot rather than build in-house

Accepted — revisit at 12 months
Related: Build vs. Buy framework · Referenced by: Risk Register R3

Context

Meridian has no in-house data science team with production fraud-model experience. SIU leadership wants faster time-to-value than a 9–12 month internal build would allow, and fraud-capture improvement is a primary initiative KPI that cannot be deferred to a later phase.

Decision

License a commercial P&C fraud-scoring API (vendor-hosted) for the pilot and first 12 months, fed with tokenized claim, policy, and claimant-history features. Wrap it behind the internal Fraud Risk Model Client component (see the Component Diagram) so the vendor can be swapped later without touching the Triage Orchestrator.

Rejected Options

  • Build in-house from day one. Rejected — a 9–12 month build with no proven in-house MLOps pipeline would delay the entire initiative past its pilot window.
  • No fraud scoring in v1; defer to Phase 2. Rejected — fraud-capture-rate improvement is a primary KPI for this initiative and cannot be deferred.

Consequences

Positive: fast time-to-value on a proven vendor model; optionality preserved via the abstraction layer. Negative: vendor lock-in risk and a per-scored-claim fee; less direct control and explainability over the underlying model, mitigated by a contract requirement for vendor-provided reason codes (see Risk Register R3).

Architecture Package Summary

ClaimsPilot AI — FNOL Intake, Triage & Straight-Through Processing Platform

1. Architecture in One Sentence

A hybrid event-driven / service-based architecture — synchronous APIs at the claimant and adjuster edges, an asynchronous Kafka-backed pipeline for extraction, triage, and routing — sized to absorb a 37x catastrophe-event surge without duplicating the existing systems of record.

$1.10

3. Key Decisions

  • ADR-001: Hybrid event-driven / service-based architecture over a monolith or full microservices.
  • ADR-002: Human-in-the-loop straight-through processing under $2,500, not full automation, for the pilot.
  • ADR-003: Confluent Cloud Kafka as the event backbone (scored 4.40/5 vs. 3.65 SQS/SNS, 3.05 Azure Service Bus).
  • ADR-004: Legacy systems remain sources of truth; ClaimsPilot AI holds only in-flight case state.
  • ADR-005: Synchronous human-facing edges, asynchronous pipeline.
  • ADR-006: Buy the fraud-scoring engine for the pilot; revisit build vs. buy at 12 months.

4. Top Risks

  • R1 — LLM/extraction misreads a claim detail (Medium likelihood, High impact): mitigated by confidence-threshold gating and the $2,500 straight-through ceiling.
  • R2 — Kafka operational unfamiliarity during a surge (Medium, High): open — game-day load test required before catastrophe-event season.
  • R4 — PII exposure via LLM prompts or logs (Medium, High): mitigated by design via field-level tokenization, pending Compliance sign-off.

Full register: Architecture Risk Register, 10 risks total.

5. Recommendation

Approved for Pilot — with conditions

The architecture is approved for the 5-office, 16-week pilot, conditional on: (1) Compliance sign-off on the PII tokenization design before go-live (Risk R4); (2) a completed Kafka game-day load test before go-live (Risk R2); (3) the fraud-scoring vendor contract executing its 90-day price-change notice clause before signature (Risk R3, see Risk Register).
