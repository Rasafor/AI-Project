Project Manager Field Guide

You are training to be an AI Solution Architect in the Colaberry Enterprise AI Leadership Accelerator. You will not run projects full-time — but you will constantly sit across the table from AI systems (and human PMs) that hand you a plan and expect you to say yes or no. This guide teaches the roughly 20% of project management discipline that lets you direct, evaluate, and approve that work with confidence, without pretending to be a career PMP.

How this guide is built Every concept here is taught twice: once as a principle, and once as a fully worked example against a single fictional initiative — the Clinical Documentation & Prior-Authorization Accelerator (CDPA) at MedCore Regional Health Network. Why Project Management Exists

A project is temporary, unique work with a defined end — as opposed to operations, which is ongoing, repeatable work. Because a project has never been done in exactly this form before, it carries uncertainty that routine operations don't: nobody has built this AI documentation assistant for this EHR at this hospital network before. Project management is the discipline that converts that uncertainty into a plan someone can be held accountable to — scope that is bounded, work that is sequenced, risk that is named in advance, and progress that can be measured against a baseline rather than a feeling.

Without it, "AI will figure it out" is not a plan — it's a hope. An AI system can generate a very convincing-looking plan in seconds. The discipline you need is not to generate plans yourself; it is to know what a correct plan must contain so you can tell the difference between a plan that will survive contact with a hospital's IT department and one that will quietly fall apart in week six.

Your job, specifically You are not the project manager. You are the person who signs off on whether the project manager — human or AI — has done the job correctly. That means you need enough fluency to ask the right questions, spot the missing pieces, and refuse to approve a plan that looks complete but isn't.

The Fixed Triangle: Scope, Schedule, Cost

Every project is constrained by three variables that trade off against each other: scope (what gets built), schedule (by when), and cost (with what budget/people). Quality sits at the center — it is what you protect when the other three are forced to move. You cannot lock all three corners and quality; something has to be allowed to flex, and a competent plan says explicitly which one.

Fig. 1 — The fixed triangle. Move any one corner and at least one other must move, or quality absorbs the damage.
CDPA applied In the case study, quality/safety is fixed — the clinical-accuracy and safety gate at Milestone M2 cannot be waived, because a hallucinated clinical note is a patient-safety incident, not a schedule inconvenience. Schedule has limited flex — a one-week slip was absorbed at Milestone M4 (see the RAID log, item I2). Cost has a defined flex band — a 4% contingency reserve exists precisely so small overruns don't force scope or quality cuts. Scope is the least flexible after quality, because the five participating clinics and the top-five-payer list were explicitly locked in the Charter.

When you review an AI-generated plan, the single highest-value question you can ask is: "Which corner of the triangle does this plan say is allowed to move, and which is fixed?" If the plan doesn't say, it hasn't actually made a decision — it has just assumed everything will go perfectly.

Work Breakdown Structure (WBS)

A WBS decomposes the total scope of a project into progressively smaller, ownable pieces of work — down to work packages small enough that one person or team can estimate, execute, and be held accountable for them. The single non-negotiable rule of a WBS is the 100% rule: the sum of the work at any level must equal 100% of the scope of the parent above it — no gaps (missing work that will surface as a surprise later) and no overlap (two teams silently building the same thing).

Good WBSWork packages sum to exactly 100% at every level. Each package has one owner, an estimate, and is small enough to track weekly.
Bad WBSPercentages don't sum to 100 (or aren't stated at all). Packages are phases like "Development" with no further breakdown — impossible to track or estimate.

See the full worked example in Document 2 — Work Breakdown Structure, where CDPA's seven Level-1 phases and their sub-packages are shown summing to exactly 100% of program scope, with an owner and an hour estimate on every line.

Critical Path & Float

The critical path is the longest chain of dependent tasks from start to finish — it determines the earliest possible completion date of the whole project. Every task on it has zero float (zero slack): if any one of them slips by a day, the entire project's end date slips by a day. Tasks not on the critical path have float — they can slip a little without moving the finish date, which is precisely why they get deprioritized first when something goes wrong elsewhere.

The most common AI-plan failure A generated plan that lists tasks and dates but never names the critical path. Without it, nobody knows which delay is an emergency and which is background noise — every slip gets treated with the same (usually excessive) alarm, or worse, the same shrug.

In CDPA, the critical path runs: Initiation & Governance → EHR Integration & Data Foundation → AI Model Configuration & Clinical Validation → Prior-Authorization Automation Build → Pilot Deployment & Multi-Clinic Rollout. Clinical Workflow & Change Management and ongoing Program Management both run in parallel with float — see the dependency network and Gantt chart in Document 3.

Milestones & Gates

A milestone marks a significant point in time — it has no duration of its own; it exists the instant its predecessor work completes. A gate is a milestone with teeth: work is not permitted to proceed past it until explicit, testable exit criteria are met and a named approver signs off. A milestone without exit criteria is decoration; a gate without a named approver is theater.

CDPA's clinical-safety gate (M2) Exit criteria: (1) ambient documentation model achieves ≥95% clinical-accuracy agreement against a five-physician review panel; (2) bias & safety evaluation completed with no critical findings open; (3) CMIO sign-off recorded. Nothing in Prior-Authorization Automation Build (Phase 5) is permitted to reach production configuration until this gate clears — it is a hard dependency, not a soft one.

RAID: Risks, Assumptions, Issues, Dependencies

RAID is the single log where a project's uncertainty is made explicit and managed instead of discovered the hard way.

RiskSomething that might happen and would hurt the project if it did. Scored by impact × likelihood, with a mitigation plan.
AssumptionSomething believed true and being planned around, without proof yet. If it turns out false, the plan is wrong.
IssueA risk that already happened — it is no longer a probability, it is a fact requiring resolution now.
DependencySomething the project needs from outside its own control (another team, a vendor, a decision) in order to proceed.
The one rule that matters Every RAID row must have exactly one owner and a date (raised, and where resolved, closed). A RAID row with no owner isn't being managed — it's being logged and forgotten. When you review an AI-generated RAID log, check ownership and dates before you check content.

The full CDPA RAID log — four risks, three assumptions, two issues, and three dependencies, every one owned and dated — is in Document 4.

RACI: Who Decides, Who Does, Who's Informed

A RACI matrix maps every significant activity against every role, using four letters:

LetterMeaning
RResponsible — does the work. Can be more than one person.
AAccountable — owns the outcome and answers for it. Exactly one per activity, always.
CConsulted — two-way input sought before the decision is made.
IInformed — told after the fact, one-way.
The one rule that matters Exactly one Accountable per row. Zero As means a decision has no owner and will stall the first time it gets hard. Two or more As means two people can each blame the other when it fails — accountability diffusion. This is the single fastest check you can run on an AI-generated RACI matrix: count the As in every row.

The full CDPA RACI matrix — twelve activities across seven roles, every row with exactly one A — is in Document 5.

Agile vs Waterfall vs Hybrid

WaterfallSequential phases, each completed and signed off before the next begins. Fits work with hard regulatory or safety gates and low tolerance for late-discovered requirements — EHR integration, compliance sign-off, clinical validation.
AgileShort, fixed-length iterations (sprints) that deliver working increments and adapt based on feedback. Fits work where requirements will genuinely evolve and fast feedback beats up-front certainty — the build-out of the prior-authorization automation module and the clinic-by-clinic rollout.
HybridWaterfall gates around the phases that are irreversible or regulated; agile sprints inside the phases that benefit from iteration. Most real enterprise AI programs — including CDPA — are hybrid, not a pure ideology.

CDPA's actual model: Initiation, EHR Integration, and AI Model Validation run as gated waterfall phases (you cannot partially validate clinical safety). Prior-Authorization Automation Build and the multi-clinic rollout run as two-week sprints, because payer rules and clinic-specific quirks are exactly the kind of thing you learn by doing, not by planning perfectly in advance. See Document 6 for the current sprint.

Velocity & Burndown

Velocity is how much work a team reliably completes per sprint, measured historically — it is the single best predictor of what they can commit to next, better than optimism. A burndown chart plots remaining work (hours or points) against time within a sprint: an "ideal" straight line from full scope to zero, against the "actual" line the team is really tracking.

Healthy burndownActual line tracks reasonably close to ideal, with normal day-to-day noise. Small bumps (newly discovered scope) are visible and explained, not hidden.
Unhealthy burndownFlat for the first week then a cliff at the end ("hockey stick") — work wasn't actually tracked, it was dumped at the deadline. Or a line that never approaches zero — chronic overcommitment.
How you use this as an architect When an AI planning agent proposes a sprint commitment, cross-check it against the team's actual historical velocity (see Document 6, which shows Sprint 13's real burndown used to size Sprint 14's committed capacity). A commitment with no velocity evidence behind it is a guess wearing a plan's clothing.

Status Reporting & RAG

RAG (Red / Amber / Green) is a shorthand health signal: GREEN on track against baseline, AMBER at risk but recoverable with attention, RED off track and needs an escalated decision now. A status report's job is not to narrate what happened — it's to force the decisions that need to be made this week to keep the project on track.

Good status report
Bad status report
States RAG per workstream against a stated baseline, with a reason for any Amber/Red.
"Everything is on track" with no baseline referenced — on track compared to what?
Lists explicit decisions needed, by whom, by when.
Pure narrative of activity with no ask, no owner, no deadline.
Shows budget/schedule variance against baseline numbers.
No numbers, or numbers with no comparison point.

The Architect's Review Lens

This is your working checklist. Run it against any plan — human-written or AI-generated — before you approve it.

  • Does the WBS sum to exactly 100% of scope, with no gaps or overlaps?
  • Is the critical path explicitly named, and does float exist and get used elsewhere?
  • Does every RAID row carry exactly one owner and at least one date?
  • Does every RACI row carry exactly one Accountable — no zero, no multiple?
  • Do estimates carry a stated buffer or confidence range, not a bare single point?
  • Do milestones and gates have explicit, testable exit criteria and a named approver?
  • Does the plan state which triangle corner is fixed and which is allowed to flex?
  • Does the status report force a decision — owner, ask, deadline — or only narrate?
  • Does the plan define what happens on failure at each major gate (retry, escalate, rollback)?
  • If this plan were regenerated from the same inputs tomorrow, would it produce a consistent result — or did today's version depend on an unrecorded assumption?
If three or more items failSend it back. A plan that fails a third of this checklist is not a plan with rough edges — it's a plan that hasn't actually been through project-management discipline, regardless of how polished the prose reads.

1. Business Case & Problem Statement

MedCore Regional Health Network operates five outpatient clinics on the Epic EHR platform. Physicians currently spend an average of 2.1 hours per day completing clinical documentation outside scheduled clinic hours. Prior-authorization requests to the network's top five payers average 6.4 days turnaround with an 18% initial denial rate, driving an estimated $3.2M in annual delayed or denied claims and contributing materially to measured physician burnout (2025 internal wellness survey: 61% report EHR burden as a top-three stressor). CDPA addresses both problems with a single integrated AI platform: ambient clinical documentation and AI-assisted prior-authorization drafting and denial-risk scoring.

3. Scope Boundaries

In scopeAll 5 MedCore outpatient clinics (Epic EHR); ambient clinical documentation for all specialties present; prior-authorization automation for the top 5 payers by claim volume (Payers A–E); physician and clinical-staff training; hypercare support for 30 days post clinic go-live.
Out of scopeInpatient/hospital documentation workflows; the 2 legacy clinics still on Athena EHR (candidate for a future phase); billing/coding automation beyond prior-authorization; a patient-facing chatbot (tracked as a separate initiative).

4. Success Criteria

  • Objectives O1–O4 met and independently verified at program close.
  • Go-live achieved at all 5 clinics with zero P1 patient-safety incidents attributable to the AI platform.
  • Program delivered within approved budget of $1,240,000 ± the 4% contingency reserve.
  • Clinical-safety gate (Milestone M2) passed with documented sign-off before any prior-authorization module reaches production configuration.

Glossary & FAQ

TermDefinition
WBSWork Breakdown Structure — hierarchical decomposition of total project scope into ownable work packages, summing to 100% at every level.
Critical PathThe longest chain of dependent tasks; determines the earliest possible finish date. Zero float on every task in the chain.
Float / SlackThe amount a task can slip without delaying the project finish date.
MilestoneA zero-duration marker of a significant point reached.
GateA milestone with explicit, testable exit criteria and a named approver; work cannot proceed past it until met.
RAIDRisks, Assumptions, Issues, Dependencies — the log where project uncertainty is tracked, owned, and dated.
RACIResponsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed — a matrix mapping activities to roles; exactly one Accountable per activity.
SprintA fixed-length iteration (commonly 2 weeks) in agile delivery, ending with a working increment.
VelocityHistorical rate of work a team reliably completes per sprint; used to size future commitments.
BurndownChart of remaining work vs time within a sprint, comparing an ideal line to the actual line.
RAG StatusRed / Amber / Green health signal against a stated baseline.
SPI / CPISchedule / Cost Performance Index — ratio of planned to actual progress or spend; above 1.0 favorable, below 1.0 unfavorable.
EAC / BACEstimate at Completion / Budget at Completion — forecast total cost vs originally approved total budget.
BaselineThe approved version of scope, schedule, or cost that actuals are measured against.
Change RequestA formal proposal to alter the approved baseline, requiring the authority defined in the Charter.
Scope CreepUnapproved, incremental expansion of scope without a corresponding change to schedule or cost.
Contingency ReserveBudget set aside in advance for known-unknown risks, released under defined authority.
HypercareThe intensified support period immediately following go-live, before returning to standard support.
DependencySomething the project needs from outside its own control in order to proceed.
StakeholderAnyone who affects, or is affected by, the project's outcome.
