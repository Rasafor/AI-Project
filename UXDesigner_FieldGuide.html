Overview & the Working Example

This guide teaches the roughly 20% of user-experience design an AI Solution Architect needs — not to push pixels by hand, but to direct, evaluate, and approve UX work an AI system produces on your behalf. You will not be graded on your Figma skills. You will be graded on judgment: can you read a persona and tell whether it's grounded in a real goal or a stock-photo demographic, spot a screen with no error state, reject a design for the right reason, and sign off on an accessibility checklist with your name on it and mean it.

Everything below is grounded in a single running example so the ideas stay concrete instead of abstract.

Working Example

Industry: Healthcare, regional multi-site health system.
Company: Meridian Health Network — 12 clinics and 3 hospitals across a mid-size metro region.
Initiative: the MyMeridian patient portal redesign — self-service appointment scheduling and referral tracking, so a patient can book a specialist follow-up and see where a referral stands without calling the office.
Why it exists: 42% of online appointment-booking attempts are abandoned before completion, and unclear referral status is a leading driver of missed specialist follow-ups. The call center absorbs the overflow, at real cost, and patients with chronic conditions are the ones most likely to fall through the gap.

Every document in this guide — the personas, the journey map, the sitemap, the wireframes, the heuristic evaluation, the accessibility checklist, the token sheet, the test plan, the executive summary — is written for this feature. That's deliberate: a UX deliverable is only as useful as it is specific to the people and the screens it describes.

The Discipline

Why UX Design Exists

A screen that looks finished is easy to generate. A screen that a 67-year-old with low digital literacy can use unaided, that fails gracefully when an insurance check errors out, and that a screen-reader user can operate end-to-end — that takes discipline. UX design is the set of practices that make an interface usable under real conditions: real eyesight, real patience, real error states, real cognitive load.

An AI model can generate a screen that looks polished in seconds — clean typography, a plausible layout, on-brand color. It has no innate stake in whether a patient managing diabetes can actually find her referral, or whether the error message when insurance verification fails is a raw code (ERR_INS_409) or a sentence a person can act on. That is exactly the gap an AI Solution Architect exists to close: you supply the user-centered discipline the model does not have by default, by directing it toward a validated design, and by refusing to approve output that skips the parts that are actually hard — the empty states, the error recovery, the accessibility math.

The Core Risk You're Managing

AI-generated UX is visually fluent, which makes it persuasive. Fluency is not usability. Your job is to separate "this looks like a finished screen" from "a first-time patient with a chronic condition can complete this task alone, on a bad day, without calling anyone."

The Discipline — The 20%

Personas & Jobs-to-be-Done

A persona grounded in demographics ("Barbara, 67, retired") tells you almost nothing actionable. A persona grounded in a Job-to-be-Done tells you exactly what to design for: the progress a person is trying to make, stated independent of age or device.

JTBD Formula

"When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome]."
Example: "When my doctor tells me I need a specialist follow-up, I want to book it without navigating confusing menus, so I can manage my condition without relying on my daughter."

Demographics can still matter — age correlates with digital literacy and eyesight, which correlate with real design constraints — but they're supporting evidence for the job, not the job itself. When you review an AI-generated persona, the first question is: if I deleted the name and photo, would this still tell me what to build? If the answer is no, it's a demographic sketch wearing a persona's clothes. See the full MyMeridian personas in Document 2.

The Discipline — The 20%

User Journeys

A user journey map traces one persona through one goal, stage by stage, capturing what they do, think, and feel — and critically, where it goes wrong. A journey map that only shows the happy path is marketing collateral, not a design tool.

The value of a journey map to an architect is that it makes the unhappy path visible and specific. It's the difference between "the booking flow could have errors" (true of every flow, and useless) and "when insurance verification fails mid-booking, the patient sees a raw error code with no next step, and the only recovery path is a phone call during business hours" (specific, testable, fixable). See Document 3 for the full MyMeridian journey, including the insurance-verification failure that drives much of the rest of this guide's remediation work.

The Discipline — The 20%

Information Architecture

Information architecture (IA) is the structural skeleton underneath the visual design — how content and features are grouped, labeled, and nested so a user can find what they need without hunting. Get the IA wrong and no amount of polish on individual screens will save the product: users abandon tasks not because a screen is ugly, but because they couldn't find it, or found it under a label that didn't make sense to them.

Rule of Thumb

Every core task should reach completion in 3 clicks or fewer from the home screen, and every label should be plain language a patient would use, not internal system terminology. "Encounters" means nothing to a patient; "Past Visits" does.

See Document 4 for the full MyMeridian sitemap, click-depth table, and a plain-language label audit that shows exactly which clinical/system terms were rejected and why.

The Discipline — The 20%

Wireframes → Mockups → Prototypes

These three artifacts are rungs of a fidelity ladder, not interchangeable terms — confusing them is one of the fastest ways to approve the wrong thing at the wrong stage.

ArtifactFidelityWhat It AnswersWhat It Deliberately Skips
WireframeLow — boxes, labels, layout onlyDoes the structure and content hierarchy make sense?Color, typography, imagery, interactivity
MockupHigh — real visual design, still staticDoes the brand-correct, on-token visual treatment work?Interactivity, motion, real data edge cases
PrototypeHigh + interactive — clickable statesDoes the flow feel right when a user actually clicks through it?Production performance, real backend data

An architect reviewing a wireframe should be arguing about structure, not color. An architect reviewing a mockup should be checking it against the token sheet, not redesigning the layout. Mixing these up wastes review cycles on the wrong questions at the wrong time. MyMeridian's Document 5 is wireframe-fidelity by design — structure and annotation, not final visual polish — because at this stage in the initiative the open question is "does the flow work," not "is the color right."

The Discipline — The 20%

Nielsen's 10 Usability Heuristics

Jakob Nielsen's heuristics are the fastest, cheapest usability check available — an expert (or a trained architect) walks each screen against these ten principles before a single real user is recruited. They don't replace usability testing; they catch the structural problems testing would otherwise burn expensive participant time discovering.

#HeuristicOne-Line Definition
H1Visibility of system statusThe system always tells the user what's happening, in reasonable time.
H2Match between system and the real worldSpeak the user's language, not internal jargon; follow real-world conventions.
H3User control and freedomSupport undo, redo, and an obvious way out of an unwanted state.
H4Consistency and standardsDon't make users wonder whether different words/icons mean the same thing.
H5Error preventionEliminate error-prone conditions before they happen, or confirm before a risky action.
H6Recognition rather than recallMake objects, actions, and options visible — don't force memory across screens.
H7Flexibility and efficiency of useAccelerators for experts that novices never have to notice.
H8Aesthetic and minimalist designNo irrelevant or rarely-needed information competing for attention.
H9Help users recognize, diagnose, and recover from errorsPlain-language errors that state the problem and a next step — no codes.
H10Help and documentationEasy to search, focused on the user's task, not exhaustive for its own sake.

See the full MyMeridian evaluation — three screens, all ten heuristics, severity-rated — in Document 6.

The Discipline — The 20%

Accessibility to WCAG 2.1 AA

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines organize around four principles — POUR: Perceivable, Operable, Understandable, Robust. Level AA is the common legal and contractual bar (ADA/Section 508 alignment in the U.S., EN 301 549 in the EU); Level AAA is stricter and rarely mandated wholesale.

PrincipleMeansMyMeridian Example
PerceivableContent is presentable in ways users can perceive — contrast, text alternatives, resizable text.Cherry Red at 3.85:1 fails for body text; must be reserved for large/bold or icon-paired use.
OperableInterface is operable — full keyboard access, enough time, no seizure triggers, clear navigation.Every slot in the appointment picker is reachable and selectable by keyboard alone, with a visible focus ring.
UnderstandableContent and operation are predictable — readable language, consistent navigation, input assistance."We couldn't confirm your insurance for this visit" replaces ERR_INS_409.
RobustContent works with current and future assistive technology.Referral status badges expose a programmatic name/role, not just a color, to screen readers.
The Number Every Architect Should Know Cold

Normal text needs 4.5:1 contrast against its background (WCAG SC 1.4.3); large text (18pt+/14pt+bold) and non-text UI boundaries need 3:1 (SC 1.4.11). This is arithmetic, not opinion — an AI design agent's claim of "on-brand and accessible" should always be independently checked against these two numbers.

See the fully computed, real-number checklist in Document 7.

The Discipline — The 20%

Visual & Interaction Hierarchy

Hierarchy is the deliberate use of size, weight, color, spacing, and position so a user's eye lands on the most important thing first — without being told to. A screen with no hierarchy forces the user to read everything to find anything; a screen with the wrong hierarchy actively misleads them.

On a MyMeridian referral card, the specialist's name and the "Book This Visit" button need to dominate — that's the job the patient came to do. The insurance-authorization fine print is real information, but it is not the first thing a patient's eye should hit. When you review an AI-generated screen, ask: what does this layout tell my eye is most important, and is that the same thing the user actually needs first? If those two answers diverge, the hierarchy is wrong even if every individual element is well-designed.

The Discipline — The 20%

Design Systems & Tokens

A design system is a shared vocabulary of reusable, named decisions. A design token is the atomic unit of that vocabulary — a name that stands in for a value. The critical distinction an architect must hold onto:

Raw Value (Reject)
background-color: #FB2832;
  • Means nothing without opening the design tool.
  • If the brand color changes, every occurrence must be found and hand-edited.
  • Nothing stops it from being used somewhere it shouldn't (body text).
Semantic Token (Approve)
background-color: color.action.primary;
  • Named for its purpose — reads as intent, not paint.
  • One definition change propagates everywhere the token is used.
  • The token's usage rule (e.g. "buttons only, 14pt+ bold text") can be documented once and enforced everywhere.

See the complete MyMeridian token sheet — color, type scale, spacing, all semantic — in Document 8.

The Discipline — The 20%

Usability Testing

Heuristic evaluation and accessibility audits are expert-driven and rule-driven — fast and cheap, but blind to anything outside the expert's own frame or the rulebook's coverage. Usability testing puts the design in front of real (or representative) users attempting real tasks, usually narrating their thoughts aloud, to surface what the first two methods structurally cannot.

MethodSpeed / CostCatchesMisses
Heuristic evaluationFast, cheapStructural violations of known usability principles.Anything outside the ten heuristics; requires an expert evaluator.
Accessibility auditFast, rule-basedWCAG conformance gaps — contrast, labels, keyboard traps.Whether the design is actually easy to use, only whether it's technically operable.
Usability testingSlow, resource-intensiveReal confusion, real hesitation, real task failure — including causes no expert predicted.Small sample sizes mean it can miss rare edge cases; requires recruiting the right participants.

The participant mix matters as much as the task list. A usability test recruited entirely from digitally fluent 25–40 year-olds will not surface the failure modes that hit Barbara. See MyMeridian's recruiting criteria, task list, and script in Document 9.

Judgment

Good vs. Bad Design

The same referral card, built two ways. Both are visually "on brand" in the sense that they use only the approved Colaberry/Meridian palette — which is exactly why an architect can't approve on brand-compliance alone. The difference is entirely in usage.

Reject
  • Cherry Red body text at ~3.85:1 contrast — fails WCAG AA (needs 4.5:1).
  • Clinical jargon ("Encounter," "PND_ELIG_02," "utilization management") a patient can't act on.
  • "Book" button measures ~22px tall — well under the 44px target-size floor for this user base.
  • No visual hierarchy: doctor name, status, and legal disclaimer all compete at similar weight.
Approve
  • White-on-cherry button label set bold at 14pt-equivalent — qualifies as "large text," clears the 3:1 threshold at 3.85:1 measured contrast.
  • Plain language: "Ready to Book," not a status code.
  • Status is carried by icon + text + color together, not color alone (readable without color vision).
  • Button measures 44px tall — meets the target-size floor.

The Architect's Review Lens

When AI-generated UX work lands in front of you, run it through these seven questions before anything else. This is the compressed version of Document 6 and Document 7 — the lens you hold up first.

#QuestionWhat a "No" Means
1Does this match the approved persona/JTBD and IA, not a plausible alternative the model preferred?Scope drift — send back to the research, not the screen.
2Is every interactive element labeled with a programmatic name/role and reachable by keyboard alone?Contract violation — Blocker under WCAG 4.1.2 / 2.1.1.
3Does the screen define an error, empty, and loading state — not just success?Incomplete by definition — not shippable.
4Does every color-conveyed meaning have a redundant non-color cue (icon, text, pattern)?Fails WCAG 1.4.1 — invisible to color-blind and low-vision users.
5Can a user recover from every error path without calling support?A dead end, not a design — see the insurance-verification failure in Document 3.
6Would a first-time patient with low digital literacy understand this screen unaided?Readability/comprehension failure — request plain-language revision.
7Does anything here cross a governance boundary (new PHI exposure, new third-party widget, pattern outside the design system)?Escalate — do not approve at the implementation level.

The Problem

42% of patients who start an online appointment-booking flow in MyMeridian abandon it before completion. Referral status is opaque — patients cannot tell whether a specialist referral has been sent, is pending insurance authorization, or is ready to book — which drives missed specialist follow-ups and pushes volume into an already-overloaded call center. Patients managing chronic conditions, who need this flow to work most reliably, are disproportionately the ones who fall through the gap.

The Approach

A research-grounded redesign of the referral and appointment-booking flow, built around two primary personas (a low-digital-literacy older adult managing a chronic condition, and a mobile-first parent booking on the go), validated against Nielsen's usability heuristics and WCAG 2.1 AA, and tested with a participant mix that deliberately includes a screen-reader user, a low-vision user, and a low-digital-literacy older adult — not just digitally fluent testers. Recruiting Criteria — Deliberately Mixed Participant Pool

A pool recruited entirely from digitally fluent adults would not surface the failure modes this redesign exists to fix. The mix below is a requirement, not a nice-to-have.

SegmentCountWhy Required

Tasks & Success Metrics

#TaskSuccess DefinitionMetrics Captured
1Find and read the status of an active referral.States the correct status and its meaning, unaided, within 60 seconds.Task success (Y/N), time on task, assists given.
2Book a follow-up visit from a "Ready to Book" referral.Reaches the confirmation screen with the correct provider and a self-selected time.Task success, time on task, error count, path taken.
3Recover from a simulated insurance-verification error mid-booking.Either books via "Book Anyway — Verify Later" or identifies the phone fallback, without abandoning the task.Task success, recovery path chosen, time to recovery, verbalized confusion (Y/N).
4Increase text size via Accessibility Preferences.Locates and applies the setting within 90 seconds, unaided.Task success, time on task, navigation path (clicks vs. dead ends).
5Send a message to the clinic about a possible rescheduling need.Composes and sends a message to the correct recipient within 90 seconds.Task success, time on task, correct-recipient rate.

Post-Task Questions (Per Task)

  • "On a scale of 1 (very difficult) to 5 (very easy), how easy was that task?"
  • "How confident are you that you completed it correctly?" (1–5)
  • "Was there any point where you weren't sure what to do next? Walk me through it."

Post-Session Questionnaire — System Usability Scale (SUS)

Standard 10-item SUS administered after all five tasks, yielding a single 0–100 score per participant. MyMeridian's baseline average was 61 (marginal); the pilot build measured 76; the target is 82.

Moderator Script — Excerpt (Task 3)

Read Verbatim

"For this next task, you're going to try booking a follow-up visit, and partway through, something is going to go wrong — that's intentional, it's part of the test, and it is not something you did incorrectly. I want you to react to it exactly as you would if this happened on your own phone at home. Talk me through what you're thinking as you go. Ready? Go ahead and start from the referral you just looked at."

Moderator note: do not intervene during Task 3 unless the participant is stuck for more than 90 seconds with visible distress. Record the exact point of confusion verbatim for the heuristic-evaluation follow-up (Document 6, H9).
